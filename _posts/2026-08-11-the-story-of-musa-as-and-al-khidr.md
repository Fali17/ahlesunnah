The Story of Mūsā AS and al-Khiḍr: The Limits of Intellect Before Divine Revelation One of the clearest demonstrations that human reason cannot independently and infallibly determine the complete moral and legal status of every particular act is found in the Qur’ānic account of Mūsā and al-Khiḍr. Abū Manṣūr al-Māturīdī himself establishes an important epistemological principle when discussing the limitations of human intellects: “The fundamental principle is that intellects are finite creations which fall short of comprehending the totality of things, and understandings are deficient in attaining the ultimate reality of matters.… Whoever seeks to comprehend them, or to attain the wisdom of Lordship without an indication from Him, wrongs the intellect and burdens it with that which he knows it is incapable of.” — al-Māturīdī, Taʾwīlāt Ahl al-Sunna, vol. 4, p. 457. This principle becomes particularly evident in the story of Mūsā and al-Khiḍr. When al-Khiḍr killed the boy, Mūsā immediately objected: “Have you killed an innocent soul without [its having killed] another soul? You have certainly done a terrible thing.” Qur’ān 18:74 From everything that was outwardly apparent to Mūsā, the act was impermissible. The boy appeared to be an innocent person, no offence warranting his execution had been publicly established, and Mūsā had not been given any revealed command permitting him to be killed. Ibn Baṭṭāl makes the epistemological significance of this episode particularly explicit. He writes that Mūsā’s objection was correct according to what was outwardly apparent, and that Mūsā was not blameworthy for making the objection. Yet when al-Khiḍr subsequently disclosed the concealed reality behind his action, the matter appeared differently: “Mūsā’s denial of al-Khiḍr for making a hole in the ship and killing the boy was correct according to what was outwardly apparent, and Mūsā was not blameworthy for that. Then, when al-Khiḍr explained its reason and meaning, the correctness that had appeared to Mūsā in his objection became error, and the error that had appeared to Mūsā in al-Khiḍr’s action became correctness.” — Ibn Baṭṭāl, Sharḥ Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, 1:200. The Qur’ān then reveals the information that had been hidden from Mūsā: “As for the boy, his parents were believers, and We feared that he would overwhelm them with transgression and disbelief. So We desired that their Lord should replace him with one better than him in purity and nearer in affection. And I did not do it on my own authority.” Qur’ān 18:80–82 The final statement is especially significant: “And I did not do it on my own authority.” Al-Khiḍr therefore did not simply follow a personal moral intuition or exercise an independent judgment contrary to revelation. His action rested upon a divine command and knowledge of circumstances concealed from Mūsā. This gives us an important distinction. Mūsā judged according to the outwardly available evidence. Given what he knew, his objection was entirely understandable and, as Ibn Baṭṭāl explicitly states, correct according to the apparent circumstances. But Mūsā did not possess all of the relevant information concerning the boy, his future effect upon his believing parents, and the divine wisdom connected with his death. Once the concealed information and divine authorization were disclosed, the apparent assessment could no longer be treated as the complete ruling concerning the matter. Thus, the episode demonstrates that there is a difference between: what appears to the intellect from the information available to it; and the complete reality of a matter as known and commanded by Allah. This is precisely where the limitation of finite human intellect becomes relevant. The intellect may recognize general principles, reason from available evidence, and judge according to what is apparent. But it cannot claim exhaustive knowledge of every hidden fact, every future consequence, every particular circumstance, and every aspect of divine wisdom. If those hidden realities can affect the ruling of a particular matter, then unaided intellect cannot make itself the final and infallible judge over revelation. Ibn Baṭṭāl therefore draws a broader theological principle from the story: Whatever Allah has made binding upon His servants through His religion and Law must be authoritative over the intellects; the intellects are not themselves to be made the authority over it. He then warns against making the finite intellect the ultimate judge over matters whose realities it cannot encompass. Created intellects have an end, whereas Allah’s knowledge has no end: “They encompass nothing of His knowledge except what He wills.” Qur’ān 2:255 The argument is therefore not that reason is useless, irrational, or incapable of recognizing anything good or evil. Rather, the argument is that reason is not omniscient. Its judgments concerning particular acts depend upon the information available to it. Revelation, however, may disclose a fact, command, exception, or divine wisdom that the intellect could not have reached independently. The story of Mūsā and al-Khiḍr provides an unusually powerful illustration of this principle. Mūsā’s initial judgment was based upon the outward reality available to him; al-Khiḍr possessed additional divinely disclosed knowledge. The difference was therefore not that Mūsā suddenly became irrational and al-Khiḍr suddenly became rational. Rather, the epistemic circumstances were different. This is also consistent with al-Māturīdī’s own admission that finite intellects cannot comprehend the totality of things or attain the ultimate realities and divine wisdoms independently of divine indication. If the intellect itself is acknowledged to be finite, then it cannot legitimately transform its own limited assessment into an infallible standard by which every divine command must be judged. Accordingly, when someone argues: “Allah could not command this particular act because my intellect determines that it is intrinsically immoral,” the question that must first be asked is: How do you know that your intellect possesses all the facts and circumstances relevant to the divine ruling? The story of Mūsā and al-Khiḍr demonstrates precisely why such certainty cannot simply be assumed. Mūsā saw an apparently innocent person being killed and objected according to the outward evidence. Yet a concealed divine command and concealed knowledge altered the complete understanding of the event. Therefore, the proper conclusion is not that revelation contradicts sound reason. Rather, it is that finite reason cannot claim exhaustive knowledge of every particular act or every divine wisdom, and consequently cannot establish itself as an infallible judge over revelation. The intellect has a place: it understands, reasons, recognizes evidence, and receives revelation. But when revelation establishes something whose complete wisdom lies beyond its apprehension, the proper response is not to reject the revelation because the intellect has failed to comprehend its entirety. The proper response is to recognize the limitation of the intellect itself. This is the fundamental lesson Ibn Baṭṭāl extracts from the story of Mūsā and al-Khiḍr what appears irrational or impermissible to a finite intellect on the basis of incomplete information cannot thereby be declared impossible for Allah to command, particularly when revelation supplies the information and authority that the intellect itself lacked.
