The Shafi‘i Position on Shaving and Trimming the Beard

The relied-upon position of the later Shafi‘i madhhab is that growing and preserving the beard is Sunnah/mustahabb, while shaving it is makruh rather than haram. This is not a modern position and it is not an isolated statement of one late scholar; it is explicitly found throughout the authoritative Shafi‘i corpus.

At the same time, a significant number of classical Shafi‘i authorities understood shaving as prohibited. The issue therefore represents a genuine internal disagreement within the madhhab, followed by a later process of tarjih through which the position of karahah rather than tahrim became the muʿtamad.

Imam al-Shafi‘i himself states in al-Umm:

«وَهُوَ وَإِنْ كَانَ فِي اللِّحْيَةِ لَا يَجُوزُ...»

“And that, even though it concerns the beard, is not permissible...”

This occurs in the discussion of shaving another person's beard without permission in Kitab al-Jirāḥ wa al-Diyāt (al-Umm 6/88). Ibn al-Rif‘ah and al-Isnawi understood this wording as evidence for prohibition, although the passage occurs within a discussion of injury and liability rather than as an independent chapter on beard grooming.

More importantly, Imam al-Shafi‘i also states in al-Umm, in the discussion of leaving iḥrām:

«وَأَحَبُّ إِلَيَّ لَوْ أَخَذَ مِنْ لِحْيَتِهِ وَشَارِبَيْهِ حَتَّى يَضَعَ مِنْ شَعْرِهِ شَيْئًا لِلَّهِ، وَإِنْ لَمْ يَفْعَلْ فَلَا شَيْءَ عَلَيْهِ...»

“It is more beloved to me that he take something from his beard and his moustache, so that he removes some of his hair for Allah; and if he does not do so, there is nothing upon him...”

Thus, the early Shafi‘i material cannot simply be reduced to an absolute prohibition on every form of trimming.

Al-Ghazali in Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn (1/143) records the disagreement concerning taking from the beard, including the practice of Ibn ʿUmar and a group of the Successors of removing what exceeded a fist. He says:

«وَالْأَمْرُ فِي هَذَا قَرِيبٌ إِنْ لَمْ يَنْتَهِ إِلَى تَقْصِيصِ اللِّحْيَةِ وَتَدْوِيرِهَا مِنَ الْجَوَانِبِ»

“The matter in this is close, so long as it does not reach the point of cutting the beard very short and rounding it from the sides.”

He also places reducing the beard among the disliked practices:

«وَفِي اللِّحْيَةِ عَشْرُ خِصَالٍ مَكْرُوهَةٌ... وَالنُّقْصَانُ مِنْهَا وَالزِّيَادَةُ»

“In the beard there are ten disliked characteristics... including decreasing from it and increasing it.”

Al-Nawawi in al-Majmūʿ (1/290–291) says:

«وَالصَّحِيحُ كَرَاهَةُ الْأَخْذِ مِنْهَا مُطْلَقًا، بَلْ يَتْرُكُهَا عَلَى حَالِهَا كَيْفَ كَانَتْ؛ لِلْحَدِيثِ الصَّحِيحِ: وَأَعْفُوا اللِّحَى»

“The sound position is that taking from it is disliked absolutely; rather, it is left as it is, whatever its condition may be, because of the authentic hadith: ‘Spare the beards.’”

In Sharḥ Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim (3/149–151), he likewise says:

«وَالْمُخْتَارُ تَرْكُ اللِّحْيَةِ عَلَى حَالِهَا وَأَلَّا يَتَعَرَّضَ لَهَا بِتَقْصِيرِ شَيْءٍ أَصْلًا»

“The preferred position is to leave the beard in its natural state and not to subject it to any shortening whatsoever.”

Thus, al-Nawawi clearly prefers leaving the beard untouched, but expresses the ruling in terms of karāhah, not taḥrīm.

The Linguistic Meaning and the Legal Force of the Commands

The principal Prophetic expressions are:

«أَعْفُوا اللِّحَى»

“Spare the beards.”

«وَفِّرُوا اللِّحَى»

“Leave the beards abundant.”

«أَوْفُوا اللِّحَى»

“Let the beards grow fully.”

«أَرْخُوا اللِّحَى»

“Let the beards hang freely.”

The linguistic meanings all point in the same direction. Iʿfāʾ carries the meaning of allowing something to increase and leaving it without interference; tawfīr means leaving something abundant and full; īfāʾ conveys completeness and fullness; and irkhāʾ means letting something hang and extend.

Therefore, the linguistic meaning is clear: the Prophet ﷺ instructed that the beard be left abundant and allowed to grow rather than reduced.

The Shafi‘i argument, however, does not require claiming that these words linguistically mean “recommended.” They do not. The important distinction is between the meaning of the command and its legal force.

This is part of the wider Shafi‘i discussion of the imperative in uṣūl al-fiqh. In Nihāyat al-Wuṣūl, Safi al-Din al-Hindi gives taʾdīb as an example of a non-obligatory use of the imperative:

«الثَّالِثُ: التَّأْدِيبُ كَقَوْلِهِ عَلَيْهِ السَّلَامُ: كُلْ مِمَّا يَلِيكَ، وَالْفَرْقُ بَيْنَهُ وَبَيْنَ النَّدْبِ فَرْقُ مَا بَيْنَ الْعَامِّ وَالْخَاصِّ؛ فَإِنَّ كُلَّ تَأْدِيبٍ مَنْدُوبٌ إِلَيْهِ»

“The third [use of the command] is taʾdīb (instruction in proper manners), such as his ﷺ saying: ‘Eat from what is near you.’ The difference between it and nadb is that between the general and the specific, for every act of taʾdīb is something recommended.”

Fakhr al-Din al-Razi in al-Maḥṣūl fī ʿIlm al-Uṣūl and al-Baydawi in Minhāj al-Wuṣūl ilā ʿIlm al-Uṣūl likewise discuss nadb and other recognized uses of the imperative, showing the broader Shafi‘i usuli principle that the form ifʿal is not restricted to one unavoidable legal meaning in every context.

So the Shafi‘i position is not:

“Iʿfāʾ means recommendation.”

Rather:

“Iʿfāʾ means leaving the beard abundant, but the command to do so can, on the basis of contextual evidence, be understood as a command of nadb rather than wujūb.”

The most direct application comes from Ibn Hajar al-Haytami in Tuḥfat al-Muḥtāj, where he says that the evidence can be understood:

«لِبَيَانِ أَنَّ الْأَمْرَ بِالتَّوْفِيرِ لِلنَّدْبِ»

“To clarify that the command to leave the beard abundant is for recommendation.”

This is the key sentence for explaining why the Shafi‘i muʿtamad treats the command as recommendation rather than obligation.

The Context of Fiṭrah and Mukhālafah

The beard is mentioned within the Prophetic discussion of fiṭrah:

«عَشْرٌ مِنَ الْفِطْرَةِ: قَصُّ الشَّارِبِ، وَإِعْفَاءُ اللِّحْيَةِ، وَالسِّوَاكُ...»

“Ten are from the fiṭrah: trimming the moustache, letting the beard grow, using the siwāk...”

Al-Nawawi in Sharḥ Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim explains that scholars differed over the meaning of fiṭrah, and transmits al-Khattabi's statement that many scholars understood it as the Sunnah. He also notes that the majority of these characteristics are not obligatory.

This is therefore a relevant contextual consideration: the command appears within the broader Prophetic discussion of bodily grooming, purification, and natural appearance.

The beard commands are also repeatedly connected with mukhālafat al-mushrikīn:

«خَالِفُوا الْمُشْرِكِينَ، وَفِّرُوا اللِّحَى، وَأَحْفُوا الشَّوَارِبَ»

“Differ from the polytheists: leave the beards abundant and trim the moustaches.”

A useful Shafi‘i parallel is the command concerning grey hair:

«إِنَّ الْيَهُودَ وَالنَّصَارَى لَا يَصْبُغُونَ فَخَالِفُوهُمْ»

“Indeed, the Jews and Christians do not dye [their hair], so differ from them.”

The imperative fa-khālifūhum is itself a command, yet Shafi‘i jurists classify dyeing grey hair as Sunnah rather than obligatory. Ibn Hajar al-ʿAsqalani in Fatḥ al-Bārī explicitly states concerning this matter that the command and prohibition are not for wujūb and taḥrīm by scholarly consensus.

This does not by itself prove the beard ruling, but it gives an important Shafi‘i example of how a Prophetic imperative concerning outward appearance and differentiation can retain its command-form while being understood as nadb rather than wujūb.

The Internal Shafi‘i Disagreement and the Muʿtamad

The prohibition position is a genuine classical Shafi‘i position.

Al-Qaffal al-Shashi in Maḥāsin al-Sharīʿah (p. 239) states:

«وَلَا يَجُوزُ حَلْقُ اللِّحْيَةِ؛ لِمَا فِيهِ مِنَ التَّشْوِيهِ، وَمَعَانِي الْمُثْلَةِ»

“It is not permissible to shave the beard because of the disfigurement and the meanings of mutilation involved in it.”

Al-Halimi in Shuʿab al-Īmān (3/79) states:

«لَا يَحِلُّ لِأَحَدٍ أَنْ يَحْلِقَ لِحْيَتَهُ وَلَا حَاجِبَيْهِ»

“It is not lawful for anyone to shave his beard or his eyebrows.”

Al-Mawardi in al-Ḥāwī al-Kabīr (13/426) likewise uses the wording:

«وَلَا يَجُوزُ أَنْ يُحْلَقَ شَعْرُ لِحْيَتِهِ»

“It is not permissible for the hair of his beard to be shaved.”

Ibn al-Rif‘ah, in his discussions in Kifāyat al-Nabīh and his related commentary, uses Imam al-Shafi‘i's statement in al-Umm as evidence for prohibition, while al-Isnawi in al-Muhimmāt (9/56) explicitly argues from al-Shafi‘i's wording that the ruling is ḥurmah. Al-Adhra‘i in al-Tawassuṭ likewise says:

«الصَّوَابُ تَحْرِيمُ حَلْقِهَا جُمْلَةً»

“The correct position is that shaving it altogether is prohibited.”

Al-Zarkashi in al-Khādim also concurs with the prohibition position.

However, the later relied-upon school moved in the opposite direction.

Ibn al-Mulaqqin in al-Iʿlām bi-Fawāʾid ʿUmdat al-Aḥkām explicitly preserves al-Halimi's opinion and then says:

«وَمَا ذَكَرَهُ فِي حَقِّ اللِّحْيَةِ حَسَنٌ، وَإِنْ كَانَ الْمَعْرُوفُ فِي الْمَذْهَبِ الْكَرَاهَةَ»

“What he stated concerning the beard is good, although the recognized position in the madhhab is karāhah.”

Zakariyya al-Ansari in Asnā al-Maṭālib explicitly describes al-Halimi's prohibition as weak:

«وَقَوْلُ الْحَلِيمِيِّ فِي مِنْهَاجِهِ: لَا يَحِلُّ لِأَحَدٍ أَنْ يَحْلِقَ لِحْيَتَهُ وَلَا حَاجِبَيْهِ ضَعِيفٌ»

“The statement of al-Halimi in his Minhāj: ‘It is not lawful for anyone to shave his beard or his eyebrows’ is weak.”

Ibn Hajar al-Haytami in Tuḥfat al-Muḥtāj (9/375–376) states that shaving and plucking the beard are among the disliked practices and explains how an earlier wording such as lā yaḥill can be reconciled with the muʿtamad:

«وَالنَّصُّ عَلَى مَا يُوَافِقُهُ إِنْ كَانَ بِلَفْظِ لَا يَحِلُّ يُحْمَلُ عَلَى ذَلِكَ... أَوْ يَحْرُمُ كَانَ خِلَافَ الْمُعْتَمَدِ»

“A textual statement agreeing with it, if it comes with the wording ‘it is not lawful,’ is interpreted accordingly [as negating unrestricted permissibility]; but if it says ‘it is prohibited,’ then that is contrary to the relied-upon position.”

Al-Ramli in Nihāyat al-Muḥtāj makes the distinction entirely explicit:

«حَلْقُ اللِّحْيَةِ مَكْرُوهٌ... وَلَيْسَ حَرَامًا»

“Shaving the beard is disliked... and it is not unlawful.”

Al-Bujayrimi in Ḥāshiyat al-Bujayrīmī ʿalā Sharḥ al-Khaṭīb (4/346) similarly states:

«أَيْ إِنَّ حَلْقَ اللِّحْيَةِ مَكْرُوهٌ حَتَّى مِنَ الرَّجُلِ وَلَيْسَ حَرَامًا»

“Meaning: shaving the beard is disliked, even for a grown man, and it is not unlawful.”

Al-Bakri al-Dimyati in Iʿānat al-Ṭālibīn (2/386) summarizes the later position and names al-Ghazali, Shaykh al-Islam Zakariyya al-Ansari, Ibn Hajar al-Haytami, al-Ramli, al-Khatib al-Shirbini and others, stating that the muʿtamad is karāhah.

Trimming and the One-Fist Question

The issue of trimming must be separated from the issue of complete shaving.

The Shafi‘i school does not establish one fist as a universally binding minimum in the same way found in the Hanafi and Hanbali discussions.

Al-Ghazali (Iḥyāʾ 1/143) records that Ibn ʿUmar and a group of the Successors removed what exceeded a fist, while other early scholars disliked doing so.

Al-Nawawi, however, adopts the stricter view:

«وَالصَّحِيحُ كَرَاهَةُ الْأَخْذِ مِنْهَا مُطْلَقًا»

“The sound position is that taking from it is disliked absolutely.”

Thus, according to al-Nawawi, even removing what exceeds a fist remains disliked outside the relevant ritual context.

Qadi ʿIyad's discussion, transmitted by al-Nawawi in Sharḥ Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim (3/151), is also important because it preserves the diversity of early practice:

«يُكْرَهُ حَلْقُهَا وَقَصُّهَا... وَأَمَّا الْأَخْذُ مِنْ طُولِهَا وَعَرْضِهَا فَحَسَنٌ»

“It is disliked to shave it and cut it... but as for taking from its length and width, that is good.

He then records disagreement over whether the beard has a fixed limit, including the view that what exceeds a fist may be removed and the view of those who disliked taking from it except in Hajj or ʿUmrah.

This demonstrates that the one-fist question is itself a historical area of disagreement, and should not simply be treated as though the Shafi‘i position is identical to the Hanafi or Hanbali formulation.

Final Conclusion

The most accurate description of the classical Shafi‘i position is therefore:

The linguistic meaning of iʿfāʾ, tawfīr, īfāʾ, and irkhāʾ is to leave the beard abundant and allow it to grow.

The Shafi‘is do not deny the authenticity of the Prophetic commands, nor do they deny their linguistic meaning.

The disagreement is over the legal force of the imperative: whether the command establishes wujūb or nadb.

The Shafi‘i usuli tradition recognizes nadb and other non-obligatory uses of the imperative; al-Razi in al-Maḥṣūl, al-Baydawi in Minhāj al-Wuṣūl, and al-Isnawi in Nihāyat al-Sūl discuss this broader principle.

The direct application to the beard appears in Ibn Hajar al-Haytami's statement that:

«الْأَمْرَ بِالتَّوْفِيرِ لِلنَّدْبِ»

“the command to leave the beard abundant is for recommendation.”

Thus, according to the later muʿtamad:

Growing the beard: Sunnah / mustahabb.

Shaving the beard: Makruh, not haram.

Trimming: Makruh according to the stricter formulation of al-Nawawi, while earlier Shafi‘i authorities such as al-Ghazali permitted taking from excessive length and recognized disagreement over the amount.

One fist: Not a binding minimum in the relied-upon Shafi‘i position.

At the same time, the prohibition position remains a genuine classical Shafi‘i view, represented by authorities such as al-Qaffal, al-Halimi, al-Mawardi, Ibn al-Rif‘ah, al-Isnawi, al-Adhra‘i and al-Zarkashi.

The final Shafi‘i position is therefore not that the beard commands are weak or that iʿfāʾ means merely “permission.” Rather, the commanded action is to leave and grow the beard, while the legal force of that command is understood by the muʿtamad as nadb rather than wujūb, and consequently shaving is classified as makruh rather than haram.
